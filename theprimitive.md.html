# The Primitive Given that primitivism stems from othering everything outside of Western culture, it’s still present in contemporary culture. Performance art / “ragebait” like Kate Shumskaya’s Instagram series I’m tired of being normal exemplifies contemporary primitivism by mocking highbrow European dining norms through eating with her hands in upscale restaurants. By behaving this way to portray her exhaustion with inhibited European culture, she implies that eating with one’s hands (as is customary with specific dishes in Ethiopia, Southeast Asia, Nigeria, etc) isn’t “normal”. As Ratcliff defines it, primitivism is “an attempt, made within the confines of ‘high culture,’ to enjoy the supposed benefits of ‘low’ or alien culture.” Primitivism is a problematic tool of resistance. Shumskaya’s series, however, is a less overt example than the white-climate-activist obsession with Indigenous people. White people hold a simultaneous fascination and degradation of Indigenous people’s customs and appearances. Speaking locally, the Coast Salish are mysticized, assumed to have ancient wisdom and a magical connection to nature. This manifests in settlers fetishizing the Coast Salish, buying dreamcatchers, and getting tattoos in the traditional geometric art style they have no connection to outside of what they assume it symbolizes–a break from Western ecocide. This ultimately undermines the effort to respect Indigenous knowledge in conservation efforts. Like Gauguin, many Canadians obsess over the aesthetic of Indigenous people without putting in effort to know individuals. Cultural iconography becomes devoid of its original meaning, transforming into a symbol of opposition to oppressive Western beliefs, but ultimately, in its usage, perpetuating those values.
